Abstract—Movement sensors are used, e.g., for automated
lighting or for HV AC automation. Depending on the type of
sensor (ultrasonic, radar, etc.), not only the presence but also the
number of people in a building can be determined. ML models
trained with movement data can support the interpretation of local
movement patterns:Situated intelligencemeans putting elementary
data, such as step sensor measurements, together and learning
bottom-up which movement patterns may indicate processes such
as crowd formation or hurried motion. Such dynamic inference
mechanisms are useful in guidance systems for key public venues,
including shopping malls or tourist attractions. Compared to
video-based automation, situated AI is more adaptable and can
also be used where filming is not allowed due to privacy. In
this work, we discuss privacy-aware tracking for environments
using floor sensors and training data we built synthetically, but
realistically, by imitating human musculoskeletal biomechanics.
For two feasibility case studies, one in a grocery store and one
on a city bus, we explain how we track unidentified people and
derive limited behavioral indicators only from foot movements in
a narrow area that we covered with a sensor map.
Index Terms—intelligent environments; smart retail; building
automation; smart transportation; situated intelligence
I. INTRODUCTION
Most people will agree that it is a good idea to align the
schedules and routes of buses and the placement of new bus
stops, taking into account the actual needs of passengers.
However, for city councils, it is not trivial to determine
where and when travelers would like to have a new stop.
Therefore, the planning of new routes is typically based on
map partitioning and demographic data such as the size of the
working population in an area [1]. Crowding around the bus
entrance or other dynamic data, such as average travel times, is
generally ignored since this information is not acquired when
only ticket sales data are collected. Therefore, no detailed
passenger behavior analysis is conducted, although it could
substantially improve the planning of bus routes [2].
Grocery store management strategies also depend on un-
derstanding customers’ needs. If store operators knew what
customers were likely to buy next, they could develop a
more forward-looking and economically effective inventory
management strategy. At the same time, they could run a more
sustainable business that avoids food waste.
Using sales data for different goods and visitor data for
different departments of a store, AI models can be trained to
make useful predictions for sustainable inventory management
strategies, which customers are likely to support [3].
However, most people will probably not agree with being
filmed on a bus or in a store to create training data for an AI,
regardless of the potential benefits to environmental protection
or urban mobility. In particular, the recording of video material
showing individuals and the digital post-processing of such
material is generally refused or even prohibited by law in some
places [4]. Therefore, tracking the behavior of people in public
places like buses is typically done by conducting surveys on
site [2]. One of the reasons why the results of such surveys are
unreliable is that many relevant groups do not participate at all.
These groups include people who are in a rush or people who
are dissatisfied and could name weaknesses that need to be
addressed. In statistics, this problem is known as the coverage
error, which has already been studied for a long time with the
aim of conducting more reliable surveys [5].
As an alternative to filming or collecting questionnaires, we
describe an approach for detecting and characterizing local
movement patterns without identifying individuals or intruding
on their privacy. To overcome the problems with unavailable
and/or sensitive data resulting from human behavior, we trained
an AI model to perform a floor-sensor-based gait analysis. The
footstep placement data which any person leaves unintentionally
wherever she or he is walking are not data in need of protection.
These data can be recorded in public places and processed
electronically without the need for asking the originator. The
data is verifiable, observed people remain anonymous and the
local tracking data allows for deriving meaningful behavioral
indicators, as explored in our experiments.
We raise the following three research questions (RQ):
RQ1: How reliably can Machine Learning (ML) detect foot-
steps from the data collected by floor sensor mats and
assign the movement events to individuals?
RQ2: Can we generate useful synthetic data for training
our model that recognizes people on sensor mats by
simulating human biomechanics?
RQ3: What installation and operational factors influence the
precision and reliability of such systems?

For a comparison with computer-supported video surveil-
lance, we describe a reference experiment on customer obser-
vation in a mall in New Zealand where public video recording
is allowed [6]. In a follow-up experiment, we installed floor
sensor mats in a German grocery store. In both experiments,
the floor sensors provided more fine-grained local movement
information where customers were counted. However, the
customer movements could not be analyzed at the same level of
detail. We conducted another experiment in a city bus, where
we also installed our floor sensor mats. The recorded timing
and movement data provided a clear understanding of where
and when the bus was empty or overcrowded.
As a preparation procedure for the sensor experiments, we
recorded ourselves walking across the sensor mats in the
case study environment. By combining the recorded data
with simulation data, we could train a reliable AI model,
although the recorded movement data, originating from two
of the authors, was limited and most input was generated
by imitating human movements. Imitated human movements
are also used for making avatars look realistic in interactive
educational software or computer games [7]. It has also been
shown that prompt reactions to human movements lead to safer
interactions between people and robots [8]. However, step
pattern imitation for privacy-aware local movement analysis
in urban environments has not yet been comprehensively
investigated elsewhere.
To address concerns regarding data collection and generation
in relation to data protection and ethics, we emphasize that all
data we collected are subject to the European Union’s General
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and New Zealand’s Privacy
Act 2020. All common legislative and ethical guidelines,
including those of the participating universities, have been
observed and complied with. Using the data, we perform a
statistical analysis and explain what the measurements tell us
about the observed movement patterns.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In
Section II, we discuss other work on interpreting behavioral
processes based on event tracking. Section III shows how
our neural network can interpret footstep events after we
trained it with data that are synthetic but not random: using a
biomechanical simulation to obtain realistic training data is the
central methodological contribution of this work. In Section IV,
we evaluate this idea in our experiments and compare the results
from automated tracking with hand-annotated datasets.
II. RELATEDWORK
Process miningis a branch of data science related to the
work we present, as it is also focused on behavioral data [9].
One problem of ERP systems, as they are often used in business
analysis and economic process mining, is that the captured
data is too coarse-grained for the computer-based detection
of behavioral patterns. One reason for gaps in the data is
that many systems retrieve data usingpull-methods importing
measurements on demand. Our floor sensors produce small
data packets, which can be allpushedto our backend, leading
to a more reliable measurement interpretation [10].
Alternatives to floor sensors that are sometimes used in
warehouses for monitoring employees are shelf sensors and
wearable devices, such as electronic wristbands [11]. While
process mining can benefit from the combination of multiple
data sources, maintaining situational awareness across an
organization becomes more difficult with the number of systems
involved [12].
Process mining systems for manufacturing, such as the ones
described above, deal with processes that have an outcome
allowing for the insertion of labels into the recorded timeline
whenever a product is finished. Similarly, recorded dialog
allows for the insertion of labels corresponding to the dialog
topics [13]. Finding appropriate labels for footstep recordings
at consecutive process states is more intricate. However, our
work is not aimed at the reduction of processing steps or at
understanding people’s opinions concerning a topic, but rather
at making beneficial adjustments to the environment which can
be done without labeling the observed processes.
Other work on improving urban living via automation focuses
on environmental characteristics and static conditions, such
as the proportion of green space in an area, which can be
captured electronically from photos without the need to track
motions [14]. In another case study that used motion tracking,
397 test participants had to agree with the installation of a
tracking app on their personal smartphones. The goal was ro
predict when one of these persons would leave a store or visit
the next one [15].

Similar to the worker-wristband study we discussed above,
all participating persons must carry some kind of transponder
device, which is not feasible outside industrial scenarios. These
sensing modalities are relevant alternatives for privacy-aware
tracking; however, an empirical comparison between footstep
analysis and tracking people carrying transponders is beyond
the scope of the case study presented in this work.
An AI model for anonymous foot localization and local
tracking, as presented in this paper, was not used in the studies
discussed above, which use various sensors and/or cameras
in combination. We only use floor sensors and our AI model
ignores identities, i.e., we focus on anonymous local movement
patterns instead of recognizing who is being tracked, in contrast
to other work that uses AI in gait analysis for recognizing
individuals [19].
III. METHODS: PRECISE PEOPLE LOCALIZATION ON FLOOR
SENSING MATS
Our method for detecting the position of people on floor
sensor mats is based on the application of AI models from
image processing to the data matrices of the floor sensor mats.
The basic principle, in image processing and in our application
as well, is the arrangement of the sensitive areas in a matrix
of rectangles. This matrix is comparable to the pixels of a two-
dimensional bitmap image. To further increase the trigger rate,
the sensitive rectangular areas are divided alternately along
the diagonals and anti-diagonals. This results in a special data
map in which each coordinate pair corresponds to two data

Their intersections with the floor are used for generating synthetic sensor data.
values: either a measurement at the bottom left and top right
or a measurement at the bottom right and top left.
The processing of data from adjacent patches works segment-
by-segment, regardless of the shape of the single patches (or
the sensor matrices they contain) which can be triangular (as
in our case) or, for example, rhombic.
The only requirement is that all shapes are polygonal and
do not overlap, as the neighborhood relationship must be
clearly defined. The resulting data are used to train aninstance
segmentation networkthat can estimate the position of a
person’s feet walking across the floor sensors by analyzing
the pattern generated on the sensor mat from multiple sensors.
Using the segmentation masks generated by the network, a
weighted average is calculated to estimate the positions of a
person’s feet, labeled F1 and F2 in Figure 1.
Regarding the design of the sensor mats, it should be noted
that it is possible and common practice to adapt them to
the floor layout without a loss of functionality [20]. In our
experiments, we used the floor sensor system “SensFloor,”
which is primarily used in German hospitals for accident
detection.
The following subsection III-A explains the process of
generating synthetic data in more detail and justifies our choices
of software technology. The implementation of the instance
segmentation network and the algorithm for calculating the
weighted mean value are discussed in more detail in the
following subsection III-B.

A. Realistic Generation of Synthetic Data
In our synthetic data generation procedure, the setting of
foot positions is based on a 3D simulation of walking humans
triggering signals on simulated SensFloor patches. We modeled
the former inUnity, due to the flexibility of this software with
respect to input and output data 1. Our patches are described
using the so-calledAsset Bundlesin theUnity Editor. We divide
3D patch models into eight triangles per patch and export the
position data to the simulation softwareBlender 2, where we
run the movement simulation. Blender dynamically finds the
regions where objects intersect, as required for locating the
footsteps.

available and document various human motion styles, e.g.,
walking and running [22].
As an alternative to the recorded data, we also considered us-
ing a physics-based model, such as thelinear inverted pendulum
(LIP). LIP was originally developed to move biped robots [23]
and allows for the introduction of cost functions and their
optimization toward more energy-efficient movements [24],
which is not relevant to us. One reason why many other
studies on footstep simulation are based on physics is that
regular movements are required. Recognizing the differences
between these and human movements is helpful in identifying
abnormalities that indicate pathological conditions [25], which
is also not relevant to our work.
Comparisons between recorded and calculated movement
data are common in medical big data analyses and often take
into account, in addition to foot movements, detailed muscle
action recordings, e.g., surface electromyography (EMG) [26].
Unlike a clinical study of human movements, our model
requires only pixel-by-pixel masks as training data. The
instance masks that represent our floor indicate pixel-by-pixel
where sensor activations occur, similar to the description of
moving objects, their edges, and contours in raster image
processing. [27].
As shown in Figure 2, we link the motion capture data
to cuboid meshes, which we use for to represent the feet of
walking people - the reason for modeling ”brick-shaped” feet is
that Unity must store a mesh for every object used in Blender
for calculating collision events. The patterns left by people
walking over the sensors on the floor predominantly depend
on the way their feet are placed relative to our triangular
sensors. For example, a single foot can activate only the two
top triangles of a patch when placed on the upper part. However,
it can potentially activate up to eight triangles, which occurs
when it is placed in the center of a patch. In the simulation,
it is necessary to ensure a good variety of foot placements in
relation to the sensors. Therefore, we randomly alter the starting
position and orientation of the simulated humans every time
we spawn another thread to simulate a new human instance in
our walking simulation. An example simulation iteration can
be seen in Figure 3, where five simulated humans walk across
a grid of 10×10 floor patches and trigger different activation
patterns.
Thus, our algorithm for creating simulated humans with
their starting position and orientation performs the following
steps: First, the number of simulated humans is chosen. This
number varies between 1 and 8 for each simulation iteration.
The algorithm then randomly assigns a starting position along
any edge of the sensor area and an orientation between
[−60◦,60 ◦] to each simulated human. The walking motion
for each simulated human is provided through the motion
capture file. When the paths of two simulated humans intersect
and both activate the same cells on the sensor floor, one of the
activations is ignored in our experiments. This simplification
reduces the realism of heavily overlapping crowd situations
and should be considered a limitation of the synthetic data
generation.
We find that combining recorded measurements with a small
amount of random noise produces suitable training data for
an AI model capable of categorizing human movement events.
This constitutes an additional result of our work.
B. Architecture and Implementation
To detect and differentiate between the feet of people walking
on the floor sensors, we use instance segmentation withpixel
embeddingsand a subsequentweighted averagecalculation
to estimate the position. These implementation decisions are
justified for our applications since sensor floor mats are costly
and, therefore, often used in small and strategically placed
areas. For our supermarket experiments, we focus on the cash
register area where two billboards are located. In the public
bus, our sensor mats are placed close to the doors. Thus, we are
processing dense data that are relevant for localized events such
as waiting near a billboard or passenger movement near the bus
doors. The embedding of pixels has shown clear advantages
in such scenarios [28] over other AI models, e.g., a Support
Vector Machine (SVM) that is suitable when a classification
involves a large number of irrelevant features [29].
We embed the individual pixels into an n-dimensional space
such that all pixels of one-foot instance are near each other and
far away from the pixels of other instances. In a subsequent
step, the pixel embeddings are clustered to assign them to
an instance. We use adiscriminative lossfunction and the
mean shift algorithmfor clustering, which have been proven
to be useful for training a reliable model without recurrent
mechanisms in computer vision [30].
Other instance segmentation networks typically have two
stages: 1) the generation of region proposals; and 2) subimage
segmentation [31]. In our case, region proposals are not reliable
because the activation patterns produced by individual feet
consist of a small number of pixels that are close to each other
and often occlude.

An advantage of the instance segmentation method with
pixel embeddings, which we implemented, is that our AI model
processes the image as a whole in one single segmentation
stage and can better handle complex occlusions. It also does
not need bounding boxes to provide additional information to
differentiate between instances.
We work with a 10×10 grid of floor patches. Our neural
network architecture is shown in Figure 4 and consists of
three convolutional layers with a mean pooling between them
on the descending branch. For the ascending branch, this
structure is mirrored, and the pooling operation is replaced by
transposed convolution for upsampling. In addition, two skip
connections are used. For the pixel embedding, 16 dimensions
are used in the last layer of the network. This network size is
sufficient for our purposes. A larger network would be harder
to train and more prone to overfitting. The embedding in the
16-dimensional space is done by calculating the loss. Instead of
soft-max, as commonly used in instance segmentation, we use a
discriminative loss function, since this loss function has shown
better performance in detecting objects that are separated into
multiple pieces, as required for our floor sensors: it pulls the
embeddings towards a cluster center, while dissimilar data are
pushed further away from each other. Our implementation of
the model is based on U-Net [32] and was trained for 200
epochs on a training dataset with 25918 synthetically generated
footstep samples.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
To motivate the two follow-up experiments showing the
usefulness of our floor sensors, we start with a smart retail
case study which we conducted in Christchurch, New Zealand,
without using floor sensors.
Christchurch is a unique area for examining the relation-
ship between pedestrian traffic and retail revenue. The city
underwent a significant redevelopment after the 2010 and
2011 earthquakes, providing an opportunity to create modern
infrastructure and amenities.
Data for our experiment were collected using surveillance
cameras from stores representing six different categories:
“apparel & personal goods”, “cafes, bars & restaurants”, “fuel
& automotive”, “groceries & liquor”, “home & recreation” and
“other retail”. The diagram in Figure 6 shows the mean daily
count of pedestrians on the x-axis. The mean expenditure in
New Zealand Dollars is shown on the y-axis. One might expect
a linear relationship; that is, more customers produce more
revenue. Obviously, such a relationship isnotpresent in our
data. Therefore, expenditures must be influenced by factors
such as alternative shopping on-line or economic resilience.
In addition to synthetic validation (Table I), we evaluated
detection quality using 49 manually annotated real images
(Table II). The result was a precision/recall = 0.887 with an
IoU of 0.95. This result suggests that our instance segmentation
model can be transferred to real mats. However, given the
limited size of the hand-annotated real dataset, this conclusion
should be interpreted as preliminary. The video study in the
mall serves only as contextual evidence of pedestrian buying
behavior. It wasnotused to train or evaluate the foot detection
model.
In the following two case studies, we not only count people
but also explore movement-related indicators that may be
associated with purchasing decisions and other local behavior
patterns. These parameters probably include the range of
available products and convenient and affordable access to
shops, including parking and transportation options. Figure 5
shows that our installation covers the entry/exit area and allows
us to observe people getting on and off the bus. In addition,
we observe who takes a seat and how many passengers remain
standing in the entry area until they get off. Figure 7 shows
the results of the first experiment we conducted for a month
with our sensor floor mat on a city bus in Regensburg.

For the next experiment, we installed floor sensors in front
of a digital billboard displaying new products at a grocery
store in Regensburg, which we observed for one month. In
our case study, we used heuristic grouping rules: whenever we
observed people walking distances less than 70 cm within a
given period, we treated this as a possible indicator of interest
in the advertisements, as they stayed close to the billboard for
a moment. In contrast, people leaving the sensor area after
3 seconds or less were treated as people who are probably
not interested in the advertisements. Figure 8 left shows a
fine-grained classification of the measurements: For durations
between 3 and 15 seconds, we discretely counted the number of
people staying in front of the digital billboard. The number of
customers who stay in front of the billboard rapidly decreases
after 5 or 6 seconds; that is, there are only a few customers
whose attention was caught for a longer time. Less than 100
customers spent more than 10 seconds there, while more than
500 stayed for only 2 seconds. This is still more than the time
required for a single step; i.e., all these customers should have
noticed the billboard.
The result of a statistical analysis of the times people stood
in front of the billboard is shown in Figure 9. We focused
on people whom our AI recognizes as being in front of the
billboard for more than 15 s. As a heuristic interpretation,
those who spent this amount of time directly in front of
the billboard are likely to have viewed the ad. Some false
positives may remain; for example, people who stood near
the billboard for longer periods for other reasons. This is
underpinned by the approximately periodic length of stay
corresponding to the duration of the advertisement running in a
loop. In Figure 9, the value ranges are grouped into bins of 10 s.
Many people turned away after 80 s, after 160 s, and around
240 s. These intervals correspond to the repetitions of the ads
shown. These observations indicate that the AI can provide a
plausible mapping between foot movements and people in this
deployment.
The average customer count for one month of opening hours
is shown in the right part of Figure 8 and was also verified
using our on-site transcript. The highest customer density
was observed during the evening, after many customers had
finished their office hours. Duration measurements resulting
from tracking customers’ feet are another valuable outcome of
our experiment. The simple counting of customers could also
have been achieved by evaluating the cash registers.
Our observation of the staying durations clearly benefits
from our AI model trained to understand foot movements. It
provides additional insight into customer movement patterns,
even when the store is crowded. Multiple values are taken
into account: revenue, staying duration, customer count, and
distances between feet (crowdedness). Thus, our data are also
suitable for apartial information decomposition[34], which
can classify values into unique, redundant, and synergistic ones.
Since, no additional video recording of the bus and the
supermarket floor map deployments exist for the experiment
time, our behavioral interpretations cannot be proven. However,
our data interpretations are plausible, even from a more distant
perspective, taking the diagrams shown in this section into
account.
V. CONCLUSION
One research question studied in this work was how
computers can analyze local movement patterns in public
spaces when camera recordings are not available. Using floor
sensor mats on a bus and in a supermarket, and training with
biomechanically simulated data, we created an AI model for
privacy-aware gait analysis. Our results suggest that ML can
detect footsteps on floor sensor mats with promising precision,

strongly affects the usefulness of the measurements. While floor

offer a privacy-preserving option for localized planning support.
Given the small annotated real dataset and the heuristic nature

behavioral findings should be understood as indicative; future
work should extend the real-world validation and compare
